A network that never sees the picture

The demo above is training a neural network from scratch, in this tab, on your device. It's learning to paint The Great Wave off Kanagawa. It is never shown the picture. Not once.

What it gets is much less: a position on the canvas, and after each attempt, one number saying how wrong it was overall. From that alone it works out the wave, the boats and Mount Fuji in the distance.

A coordinate in, a color out

Every pixel is a question. Take the point (x, y), both running from −1 to 1, and answer with a color (r, g, b). The image is 128 × 85, so that's 10,880 questions, asked all at once.

The important part: the network doesn't store 10,880 colors. It's one function, f(x, y) → color, built from 66,307 adjustable numbers. Every change to those numbers changes the whole picture at once. That's why it starts as a smooth smear and sharpens everywhere together, instead of filling in pixel by pixel.

What "training" actually means

Training is a loop with four steps:

  1. Guess. Paint every pixel with the current numbers.
  2. Measure. Compare with the original: the average of the squared differences (mean squared error).
  3. Nudge. For each of the 66,307 numbers, work out which direction makes the error smaller, and move it a little (the Adam optimizer, step size 0.001).
  4. Repeat.

That's all. No rules about waves or mountains anywhere. The whole model is about 180 lines of TypeScript.

Why it gets stuck in a blur

Give a plain network the raw (x, y) and it learns the broad strokes, then stalls. Neural networks are biased towards smooth functions (it's called spectral bias), and the sharp edges of a woodblock print are the opposite of smooth. Switch the demo to Without and watch it plateau. In my runs it sat around 19 dB PSNR after 3,000 steps; with the fix below it passes 40 dB in about 1,000.

The fix is Fourier features (Tancik et al., 2020). Instead of handing the network (x, y), project the coordinate onto 128 random sine waves at different angles and speeds, and give it the sine and cosine of each: 256 numbers per pixel instead of 2. Those are the striped tiles in the equation above. Fine detail is now a matter of combining stripes.

You might expect the painting to be a sum of those waves, like a Fourier series. It isn't, and you can see it here: hide the fastest waves and the picture turns grainy, not blurry. I also built a true Fourier series from the same number of waves; even with all of them it's a soft blob. The network gets a sharp print out of the same stripes because it mixes them through its layers instead of adding them up.

The chart is a monitoring dashboard

The chart is the part I care about most. Without that number the loop has nothing to follow. Same as in production: if you can't measure the error, nothing gets better.

Which number turned out to matter too. I started with the raw error on a log scale: precise, but only readable if you're used to log axes. So I switched to something anyone can read, the share of pixels painted right. Then came the definition of "right". With a ±5% tolerance per color channel, my laptop reached 99% in 965 steps (33 seconds) and the chart went flat while the picture was still getting sharper. At ±1% (about ±2.5 levels out of 255) it keeps climbing for thousands of steps. Same model, same run, a different story depending on the threshold. Pick one that still moves when the thing you care about moves.

Running on your device

No GPU server, no Python: a static page and TensorFlow.js. Your device does all the work, which meant I had to care about your device:

  • A frame budget. The loop runs as many steps as fit in about 30 ms, then hands control back so the page stays responsive.
  • Paint every 10 steps, not every step. Reading pixels back from the GPU is the slow part.
  • Pause when the tab is hidden (Page Visibility API). Nobody's watching; don't burn the battery.
  • Free GPU memory. Every intermediate tensor is disposed, so memory stays flat however long it runs.

The bug that only showed up on a real GPU

The first run on my laptop (an RTX 3050, in Chrome) learned about four times slower per step than the same code on a CPU: half the pixels right at step ~600 instead of ~160.

Rather than guess, I built a check into the page (the Run diagnostics button at the bottom): a short training run with a fixed seed, whose error after steps 1, 2, 5… 100 is compared number by number against a reference run on the CPU. On WebGL the error after 100 steps was 5× the reference. On WebGPU, same GPU and same code, it matched within 0.2% and ran 3× faster: 28 ms per step instead of 86.

So the page now uses WebGPU first, and falls back to WebGL, WebAssembly and plain CPU in that order. When "works on my machine" stops being true, compare against a known-good reference and find the first number that diverges.

One function, two prints

Add one more input, t, and the same kind of network learns two pictures: The Great Wave at t = 0, Red Fuji at t = 1. It's only ever trained on those two values. Ask for anything in between and it has to make something up.

I hoped for a morph, the wave rising into the mountain. What it paints is closer to a double exposure: both prints overlaid and grainy, the sky turning blue before the wave fades. Nothing in the training says what "in between" should look like, so this is what the function happens to do there.

To make it playful, type a four-letter word. A small rule, not the network, turns it into t letter by letter: each letter lands between the WAVE letter and the FUJI letter in the same position, by distance in the alphabet. WEVE gives 0.05, FERI gives 0.63. Text in, a number out, a picture out: a toy version of how text-to-image models are wired, minus everything that makes them good.

Try it: f(x, y, t)

WAVE is the wave, FUJI is the mountain, anything else lands in between. Train first for a few thousand steps.

Is this compression?

The function can repaint the whole print, so is it a compressed image? Not here. Stored as 32-bit floats, the network's 66,307 numbers take 259 KB. The PNG it learned from is 27.5 KB, and a JPEG at quality 75 is 3.7 KB. Research on this idea (COIN, Dupont et al., 2021) gets competitive with JPEG at low bitrates using much smaller networks and quantized weights, but at 128 × 85 pixels a JPEG is hard to beat.

What you do get is an image that is a continuous function. The readout under the painting gives the color at any point you point at, including between pixels, at a resolution that never existed.

What didn't work

Two standard tricks, measured on a CPU with the ±1% threshold:

  • Shrinking the step size over time. Worse every way I scheduled it. Shrinking from the start: 45% of pixels right at step 3,000, against 93% with a constant step. Shrinking late (from step 1,500 or 2,000): 90–91% at step 4,000, against 96%.
  • Coarse-to-fine, unlocking the waves from slow to fast. It ends up in the same place (94.3% vs 93.0% at step 3,000) and gets going more slowly. It's in the code, switched off.

The plain setup won: a constant step size, all the waves from step one.
